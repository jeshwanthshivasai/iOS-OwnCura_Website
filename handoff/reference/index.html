The ownership infrastructure for American healthcare practices.

OwnCura owns and operates medical practices across the United States.

The practice is listed.

It appears on a business-for-sale marketplace, between a car wash and a franchise restaurant.

A buyer registers interest.

One button. The buyer has done this a hundred times; this is the owner's first.

Emails are exchanged.

Through a broker, who is paid when the practice sells and not otherwise.

An NDA is signed.

The owner is now in a process, and still has not been told what the practice is worth.

The CIM is prepared and sent.

Twenty years of work compressed into a document written for buyers to skim.

The EBITDA meeting.

The number is discussed for the first time, in a room where one side has done this before.

How a practice is sold today.

Six steps, and the owner learns what their practice is worth at the last one.

Why doctors sell.

Physicians cite three reasons for selling.

Each of those gets priced. The thing they are actually worried about does not.

Retirement. Thirty years in, and no one to hand the keys to.
Liquidity. The practice is the retirement plan, and it is illiquid.
Administration. Two jobs, and only one of them is medicine.
“Whoever takes it should handle it the way I did.”

You keep the practice, the patients and the decisions. We take the work that has nothing to do with medicine: billing and collections, prior authorizations, credentialing, payroll, compliance, on software proven in our own practices first.

Growth is a financing problem and a purchasing problem. We bring both: capital for a second location or an additional provider, and the purchasing terms a single practice cannot negotiate alone — supplies, labs, insurance, software.

Take some value out now without leaving. We buy a share, you keep a share, and you carry on seeing patients with none of the administration. This is also the answer when a co-owner wants out and you would rather not fund the whole buyout yourself.

We buy the practice and we operate it afterwards. No brokerage fee, because there is no broker. No minimum revenue and no minimum EBITDA to be worth our time — a two-provider practice is a practice.
